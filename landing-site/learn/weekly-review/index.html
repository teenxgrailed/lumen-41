How to do a weekly review

A weekly review is a 30–45 minute appointment with yourself to get clear (empty every inbox), get current (check calendar, projects and waiting-fors) and get creative (pick what matters next week). The idea comes from David Allen’s Getting Things Done; here is a practical version you can run on Friday afternoon.

Where the weekly review comes from

The weekly review is a core habit of Getting Things Done (GTD), the method David Allen published in 2001. Allen calls it the “critical success factor” for making the system stick (David Allen on the Weekly Review). His official checklist groups the steps into three phases: Get Clear, Get Current and Get Creative (GTD Weekly Review checklist, PDF).

You don’t need to adopt all of GTD to benefit. The review works with any planner, because its job is simple: make sure nothing important is hiding, then decide what next week is for.

The 30-minute review, step by step

Get clear (10 min)

  1. Collect loose ends (3 min). Notes on paper, screenshots, voice memos, sticky notes. Turn each into a task or delete it.
  2. Get inboxes to zero-ish (5 min). Not answering everything; deciding. Each email that needs action becomes a task with an estimate. Archive the rest.
  3. Empty your head (2 min). Write down anything still nagging: “call landlord”, “renew domain”.

Get current (12 min)

  1. Look back at last week’s calendar (3 min). Any meeting that created a follow-up you didn’t capture?
  2. Look ahead two weeks (3 min). Deadlines, trips, birthdays. Each one that needs prep gets a task now.
  3. Check every active project (4 min). Each should have at least one next action. A project with no next action is stuck.
  4. Check waiting-fors (2 min). Who owes you a reply? Add a follow-up task for anything older than a week.

Get creative (8 min)

  1. Scan your someday list (3 min). Anything ready to become real? Anything to delete?
  2. Pick next week’s top three (5 min). Three outcomes, not thirty tasks. These get the best slots when you plan your week.
Put the review itself on your calendar as a recurring 45-minute block. Allen suggests scheduling it for the next four Fridays until it becomes a habit. A fixed time and place is also an implementation intention, the kind of if-then plan research links to follow-through (Gollwitzer, 1999).

The 10-minute emergency version

Some weeks you won’t have 30 minutes. Don’t skip; shrink. This version keeps the parts that prevent dropped balls:

  1. Calendar back (2 min). Scan last week’s meetings for promises you made.
  2. Calendar forward (3 min). Scan the next 14 days for deadlines that need prep.
  3. Inbox triage (3 min). Star or task only what needs action this week.
  4. Top three (2 min). Write next week’s three outcomes.

Do the full review the following week. Two emergency versions in a row are a sign the review slot itself needs to move. If you prefer to see review and planning on one page, the weekly planner has room for both.

Common mistakes

  • Doing the work during the review. Answering emails turns 30 minutes into three hours. Capture now, do later.
  • Skipping the look-back. Most dropped balls come from last week’s meetings, not next week’s.
  • Picking ten priorities. Ten is a list. Three is a plan.
  • Reviewing when exhausted. Friday 18:00 after a long week rarely works. Try Friday 15:00 or Monday 08:30.
  • Making it perfect. A rough 20-minute review every week beats a perfect 90-minute one once a month.

Questions, answered.

How long should a weekly review take?

30–45 minutes once you have the habit. The first few may take an hour because there is more to clear.

When is the best time for a weekly review?

Friday afternoon is common because the week is fresh in mind. Monday morning works if Fridays are chaotic. Pick one and make it recurring.

Who invented the weekly review?

It is a core part of David Allen’s Getting Things Done method, which formalized it as a checklist in three phases: get clear, get current, get creative.

What’s the difference between a weekly review and weekly planning?

The review clears and checks; planning places work into next week’s calendar. Do the review first, then plan.

What if I skip a week?

Do a shorter one next time: inbox, calendar look-back, top three. Fifteen minutes is enough to get back on track.

Can I do a weekly review with a team?

Yes, but keep it separate from your personal review. A team review checks shared projects and blockers; your own review covers everything else, including personal commitments.
