Plan your week in 15 minutes

To plan your week in 15 minutes: pick three outcomes, look at the meetings already booked, estimate the tasks that serve those outcomes, block the biggest ones first, and leave about a fifth of your time open. Do it once, Sunday evening or Monday morning, and your daily plans get much shorter.

Why plan the week, not just the day

A day is too short to fit a 4-hour project around meetings. A week isn’t. Cal Newport plans his week every Monday morning and treats daily plans as the detail inside it (Plan your week in advance). Weekly planning answers the question daily planning can’t: when this week will the big thing happen?

If you haven’t cleared last week’s loose ends, do a weekly review first. Planning on top of a messy inbox just moves the mess.

The 15-minute plan, timed

  1. Minutes 0–3: Write three outcomes. Finished states, not activities. “Homepage v1 live”, not “work on website”.
  2. Minutes 3–5: Look at the fixed week. Open your calendar in week view. Count meeting hours. Note the day with the biggest open stretch.
  3. Minutes 5–9: List and estimate the tasks behind each outcome. Use 15, 30, 60, 90 or 120 minutes. Anything over 2 hours gets split.
  4. Minutes 9–13: Block the big rocks first. Put the largest task for each outcome into the longest open stretches. Smaller tasks fill in later, day by day.
  5. Minutes 13–15: Run a capacity check. Add up hours: meetings + blocked tasks. If it’s over 80% of your working hours, cut or move something now. The planning fallacy means your estimates are probably low already.
Capacity check, quick math: 40 working hours × 0.8 = 32 plannable hours. If meetings take 11, you have about 21 hours for tasks. Not 40.

Variation: theme your days

If you juggle several roles, give some days a theme. Theming cuts context switching because you stay in one kind of work longer. A simple pattern for Alex:

DayThemeWhat goes there
MonBuildWebsite deep work, no calls before 12:00
TueClientsClient calls, revisions, invoices
WedMeetingsCalls batched; light admin between
ThuBuildWebsite build + test
FriShop + reviewShop orders, content, weekly review

Themes are defaults, not rules. When a client can only meet Monday, take the call and keep the rest of the day on theme. Founders and freelancers often find this easier than hour-by-hour plans; see Needt for founders and for freelancers.

Sunday or Monday? Pick by trigger

Either works. What matters is that the plan happens at a fixed time and place, attached to something you already do. “After Sunday dinner, I plan the week at the kitchen table” is an if-then plan, the kind Gollwitzer’s research on implementation intentions links to better follow-through.

  • Sunday evening suits people who sleep better with Monday already decided. Keep it to 15 minutes so it doesn’t eat the weekend.
  • Monday morning suits people whose weekends are unpredictable, or whose Monday inbox changes priorities. Plan before you open email.
  • Friday afternoon, right after a weekly review, is the most thorough option because last week is still fresh.

Whichever you choose, add it as a recurring 15-minute event so it survives busy weeks.

Common mistakes

  • Planning every task for every day on Sunday. You’ll re-plan it all by Tuesday. Fix the big rocks; leave the rest flexible.
  • Outcomes that are activities. “Work on Shop” can’t be finished. “Autumn product list published” can.
  • No capacity check. The most common reason a week fails is that it never fit.
  • Ignoring the meeting-heavy day. Don’t block deep work on the day with 3.5 hours of calls.
  • Planning too late on Sunday. Tired planning is optimistic planning.

Questions, answered.

When should I plan my week?

Sunday evening or Monday morning. Monday works well if weekends are unpredictable; Sunday works if you like starting the week already decided.

How many priorities should a week have?

Three outcomes is a good default. One per major area of your life or work keeps it balanced.

How much of my week should I schedule?

About 80% of working hours at most, meetings included. The rest absorbs overruns and surprises.

What if the week falls apart by Wednesday?

Re-run the capacity check for the remaining days and cut one outcome down. A smaller finished outcome beats three half-done ones.

Do I need a weekly review too?

It helps. The review clears last week; planning shapes the next. Together they take 45–60 minutes.
