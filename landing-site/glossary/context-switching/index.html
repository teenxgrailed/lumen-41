Glossary / Context switching

What is context switching?

Context switching is moving your attention from one task to another. Each switch has a cost: your mind has to unload one set of goals and rules and load another, and that takes time and focus.

The definition, expanded

Switching isn’t only about big jumps. Checking a message during a design task is a switch. So is a meeting in the middle of a writing block. The cost shows up as slower restarts, lost threads (“where was I?”), and small errors. What we call multitasking is usually rapid switching, not doing two things at once.

Where it comes from

The term comes from computing, where a processor saves the state of one process and loads another. Psychologists study the human version as task switching. Rubinstein, Meyer and Evans (2001) measured time lost when people switched between tasks and found it grew with task complexity. In an office study, Mark, Gudith and Klocke (2008) found interrupted people compensated by working faster, but reported more stress and time pressure.

How to reduce it

  1. Count your switches for one morning. Most people are surprised.
  2. Batch similar small tasks so they share one switch.
  3. Give large tasks blocks long enough to finish a meaningful piece.
  4. Before stopping, write one line: the next step. It makes the restart cheaper.
  5. Cluster meetings on fewer days or at day edges.

An example

Alex’s old Tuesday: 09:00 code, 09:20 Slack, 09:35 code, 10:00 standup, 10:30 code, 10:50 client email, 11:15 Spanish homework, 11:40 code. Seven switches before noon, and the feature didn’t ship.

New Tuesday: 09:00–10:00 code (Website), 10:00 standup, 10:30–12:00 code, 12:00–12:30 messages batch, 17:30 Spanish. Two switches before noon.

Where it gets complicated

Some switching is the job: a project manager or support lead who never switches isn’t doing their work. Short breaks to a different kind of task can also help after long, tiring focus. The goal isn’t zero switches. It is fewer accidental ones, and switches that happen at natural stopping points.

Questions, answered.

Is multitasking the same as context switching?

For most knowledge work, yes. Doing two attention-heavy tasks at once usually means switching rapidly between them, with a cost each time.

How long does it take to refocus after an interruption?

It depends on the task and the interruption. A specific minute figure is often quoted online, but it varies by study and setting, so treat any single number with caution.

Do notifications count as context switches?

Even glancing at one pulls attention away. Turning notifications off during focus blocks is the cheapest fix.

Is context switching worse for some people?

Experiences vary. Many people with ADHD describe switching and restarting as especially costly, which is one reason structured blocks help some of them.
