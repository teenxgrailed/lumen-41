Glossary / Pomodoro Technique

What is the Pomodoro Technique?

The Pomodoro Technique is working in 25-minute focused intervals separated by short breaks. One interval is a pomodoro; after four, you take a longer break.

The definition, expanded

A pomodoro is indivisible. If someone interrupts you at minute 12, you either note the request and return to the task, or you abandon that pomodoro and start a new one. Half-pomodoros don’t count. That rule is what makes the method more than a timer: it trains you to defer interruptions.

Cirillo also pairs it with planning and tracking. You estimate tasks in pomodoros and record how many each actually took.

Where it comes from

Francesco Cirillo developed the method in the late 1980s while studying at university, using a tomato-shaped kitchen timer. Pomodoro is Italian for tomato. He published it as a book in 2006 and a revised edition in 2018. The physical timer was deliberate: winding it is a small commitment to start.

How it works

  1. Pick one task.
  2. Set a timer for 25 minutes and work only on that task.
  3. When it rings, mark one pomodoro and take a 5-minute break.
  4. After four pomodoros, take a 15 to 30-minute break.
  5. Log interruptions instead of acting on them, and handle them in a break.

An example

Alex has a Spanish vocab review and a client report on Thursday morning:

  • 09:00–09:25 · Report section 1 #Clients
  • 09:30–09:55 · Report section 2
  • 10:00–10:25 · Report charts
  • 10:30–10:55 · Report proofread
  • 11:00–11:25 · Long break
  • 11:25–11:50 · Spanish vocab #Spanish

A Slack message arrives at 09:41. Alex writes “reply to Sam about invoice” on paper and answers it at 09:55.

When it doesn’t work

Twenty-five minutes is short for work with a long ramp-up, like writing code or editing video; the break can land just as you reach flow. Many people adjust to 50/10 or 90/20, which works but drifts from the original method. It also doesn’t fit meeting-heavy days or roles where interruptions are the job. Research is limited: Biwer et al. (2023) found Pomodoro breaks were linked to less fatigue than self-chosen breaks in a student study, but no difference in task completion.

Questions, answered.

Why 25 minutes?

It was the length that worked for Cirillo; short enough to start without resistance, long enough to make progress. Nothing magic about it, and many people adjust it.

Pomodoro vs timeboxing?

Pomodoro is a fixed-length timebox with built-in breaks and a rule against interruptions. General timeboxing lets you choose any length and doesn’t prescribe breaks.

What do I do in the 5-minute break?

Something away from the task and away from feeds: stand up, water, look out of a window. Checking messages tends to stretch the break.

Is the Pomodoro Technique good for ADHD?

Some people with ADHD find short, defined intervals easier to start; others find the timer stressful. It is a technique to try, not a treatment.
