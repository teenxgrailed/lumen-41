Glossary / Eisenhower Matrix

What is the Eisenhower Matrix?

The Eisenhower Matrix sorts every task on two questions: is it urgent, and is it important? The answers put it in one of four boxes, and each box has one action: do, schedule, delegate, or drop.

The Eisenhower Matrix is a two-by-two grid that sorts tasks by whether they are urgent and whether they are important, giving four actions: do now, schedule, delegate, or drop.Origin: Named after Dwight D. Eisenhower, who in a 1954 speech quoted a college president on urgent vs important problems; the grid itself was popularized later, notably by Stephen Covey (1989).

The definition, expanded

  • Urgent and important: do it today.
  • Important, not urgent: schedule it. This is where planning, learning and prevention live.
  • Urgent, not important: delegate it or keep it short.
  • Neither: drop it.

The value is in the second box. Most people handle box one well already. The matrix exists to protect box two from being crowded out.

Where it comes from

On August 19, 1954, Eisenhower told the World Council of Churches in Evanston, Illinois, that a former college president had said: “I have two kinds of problems, the urgent and the important. The urgent are not important, and the important are never urgent.” The full transcript is public. Eisenhower never drew a grid. The four-quadrant version spread decades later, most widely through Stephen Covey’s 1989 book The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People.

How it works

  1. Write down everything on your plate.
  2. For each task, ask: does something bad happen if this waits a week? That is urgency.
  3. Ask: does this move a goal I care about? That is importance.
  4. Place it in a box and take that box’s action right away.
  5. Repeat weekly; tasks move between boxes as deadlines approach.

An example

Alex sorts Monday’s list:

  • Do: fix the broken checkout on the Shop, 60 min, today
  • Schedule: Spanish lesson prep, 45 min, Wednesday 08:00
  • Delegate: reformat 40 product photos, ask a freelancer
  • Drop: redesign the email signature

The Spanish prep is the one that would have slipped. Now it has a time.

When it doesn’t work

The matrix assumes you can delegate; solo workers often can’t, so box three becomes “do it fast”. It says nothing about how long tasks take or when they fit, so a list sorted perfectly can still be impossible to finish. And “important” is a judgment call that drifts toward whatever feels stressful. Pair it with estimates and a calendar.

Questions, answered.

Did Eisenhower invent the Eisenhower Matrix?

Not the grid. He quoted the urgent-vs-important idea in a 1954 speech, attributing it to a former college president. The four-box matrix was developed and popularized later.

What goes in the important but not urgent box?

Work that pays off later and never shouts: planning, learning, health, maintenance, relationships. It is the box most worth protecting with calendar time.

Eisenhower Matrix vs Ivy Lee method?

The matrix sorts many tasks into categories. Ivy Lee picks the six most important tasks for tomorrow and ranks them. Many people sort with the matrix weekly and pick daily with Ivy Lee.

How often should I redo the matrix?

Weekly is enough for most people, plus a quick check when a big new task arrives.
